Pronuclear Structure Clock loading…

How we read developmental time off a fixed 3-D zygote

A fixed MERFISH stack carries no timestamp. This walks through how the embryo's own 3-D structure — cell body, pronuclei, polar body — is turned into a normalized developmental position, how the clock was trained and tested on live-imaged embryos, and how far the answer can be trusted on each of our own zygotes. Every number is read from the frozen model artifacts.

A structure-aware geometry-to-time calibration: segmentation → dimensionless geometry → a monotone probabilistic clock. It is not a mechanistic model of pronuclear migration, and it makes no claim about the forces involved.

1

The developmental clock we are estimating

Every zygote is placed on the same 0→1 scale, so embryos of different absolute duration are comparable.

2

What we actually get: one static 3-D stack

Pick any embryo. The three orthogonal planes are all the evidence there is — no before, no after.

3

Step one: find the structures, and check them

Segment identity is decided by geometry, never by a label name — the files only say “Segment_1…4”.

4

Step two: reduce it to one dimensionless number

Distances are divided by that embryo's own cell radius, so the model never depends on microns-per-pixel or embryo size.

5

Step three: the clock, learned from embryos whose time is known

A monotone curve fit to 53 live-imaged zygotes — the only cohort where developmental time was actually measured.

6

Does it work? Held-out embryos say how well

Leave-one-embryo-out cross-validation, plus a check that the stated intervals really contain the truth that often.

7

Every one of our zygotes gets an answer

Including the awkward ones. Nothing is dropped; what is uncertain is marked uncertain.
