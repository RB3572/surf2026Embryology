Pick a zygote
A green tab has a labelled sperm and gets its real sperm plane. The rest get a pseudosperm plane — the meridional angle at which the template genes best reproduce the agreements and oppositions they showed across the real sperm planes. The template is set by a P cutoff, not by picking genes.
Cross-section at the plane
The cytoplasm outline in the plane's own frame, looking down the COM → polar-body axis. The cut runs vertically and the template genes' transcripts are coloured by which side they fall on. Pronuclei and the polar body are separate segments and enter no count, no volume, and nothing drawn here.
Template score across every angle
Every plane containing the COM → polar-body axis, swept in 1° steps. The score is the weighted mean of sign × log₂ fold over the template genes — each gene's own sperm-plane direction, weighted by −log₁₀P — so the peak is the plane where they best reproduce it. Read the shape, not just the peak: a flat curve means the angle is barely determined, and a bare number would hide that.
Which template genes agree here
Each template gene's sign × log₂ fold at this plane. Positive (teal) means it lands on the same side of the bulk transcript gradient it took across the real sperm planes; negative (red) means it opposes. The F or E after a gene name is the side it wants — the fuller or the emptier half. The fit maximises the weighted mean of these bars.
Does the fit find the real sperm plane?
The only honest test of this whole idea. For the zygotes that do have a sperm, the template fit is run anyway — ignoring the sperm — and the angle it lands on is compared with the plane the sperm actually defines. Chance is a flat line: two planes drawn at random differ by 45° on average, so 45° is the null, not 0°.
